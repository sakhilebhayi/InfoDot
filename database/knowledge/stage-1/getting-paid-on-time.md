Work delivered, invoice sent, and the customer's 30 days quietly becomes 60 or 90 — while you keep supplying because you are afraid of losing them. Late payment is not solved by hoping; it is solved by a credit-control ladder: vet before you give credit, agree terms in writing, invoice immediately, chase on a fixed timetable, and escalate when nothing else works. Setting the ladder up takes about a week; after that it mostly runs itself.

## The problem
Late payment starves a business of cash it has already earned. In South Africa it is structural: National Treasury's own compliance tracking showed 95,399 government invoices older than 30 days, worth R12.4 billion, unpaid at the end of the second quarter of 2025 — even though the Public Finance Management Act and Treasury rules require departments to pay valid invoices within 30 days. Private customers are not reliably better: Xero's small-business data for Australia (June 2026 quarter) measured an average 22.9 days to get paid, with payments on average 6 days late and 41% of businesses waiting more than two weeks past due. No equivalent verified South African debtor-days figure exists, but the mechanics are the same.

The usual root causes sit on the seller's side of the fence, which is the good news, because those you can fix: credit given without vetting, terms never agreed in writing, invoices that go out late or with errors, and no systematic follow-up.

## Who this is for
Any business that sells on credit: services, trades, suppliers to corporates or government. One South African detail decides your escalation route: the Small Claims Court only accepts claims brought by natural persons, so a sole proprietor can sue there in person, while a company or close corporation must use a letter of demand, an attorney or the Magistrates' Court instead.

## The approach and why it works
A credit-control ladder, climbed in order: (1) vet the customer, (2) agree written terms, (3) invoice immediately and correctly, (4) make paying easy, (5) remind on a fixed escalating timetable, (6) enforce consequences, (7) formal demand and legal recovery. Each rung is cheap and most debts never reach the top. This is the standard credit-management practice professional bodies such as ACCA document for small businesses, and payment-platform data supports the early rungs: customers pay faster where terms were agreed up front and an easy payment method is attached to the invoice.

## Step 1 — Vet before you give credit
Before extending credit, ask for trade references, run a credit check, and set a credit limit you could afford to lose. Put it on a one-page credit application the customer signs. A customer who resists giving references is telling you something.

## Step 2 — Agree terms in writing before work starts
The quote or contract states the price, the payment term (consider 14 days rather than the reflexive 30) and — important in South Africa — an agreed interest rate on overdue amounts. Without an agreed rate you are limited to mora interest at the prescribed statutory rate, which is set at the Reserve Bank repo rate plus 3.5% and changes when the repo rate changes (it was 10.25% a year from 1 March 2026 — check the latest notice before calculating). Interest at the prescribed rate is simple interest, and the rate in force when the debt falls due stays fixed for that debt. Where the National Credit Act applies, its limits apply too.

## Step 3 — Invoice the same day, completely and correctly
Invoice the day you deliver. The invoice carries the correct legal entity, the customer's order number, your banking details, the amount and the due date. For government customers, keep proof that the invoice was valid and when it was submitted — the 30-day clock the PFMA imposes runs on valid invoices, and a sloppy invoice never starts it.

## Step 4 — Make paying easy
Offer EFT plus, where possible, an online payment link or debit order. Xero reports that invoices with online payment attached are paid up to twice as fast — a vendor's claim about its own feature, but the direction of the effect is common sense: every extra step a customer must take is a day you wait.

## Step 5 — Chase on a fixed ladder, largest debtors first
Put the reminders in the diary or automate them in your accounting package: a polite reminder 1–3 days after due date; a phone call plus a formal written reminder at 14 days; a letter of demand at 30 days overdue. Work the age analysis weekly, starting with the largest amounts, and record every promise to pay with a date you then hold them to.

## Step 6 — Enforce consequences
While an account is overdue: stop further supply on credit and apply the agreed interest. Continuing to deliver to a non-payer converts your working capital into their free loan, and they know it.

## Step 7 — Letter of demand, then the Small Claims Court or an attorney
The letter of demand states the facts and the exact amount, and is delivered by hand (get a signature), by registered mail or by the sheriff. If you are a natural person and the debt is within the limit, the Small Claims Court at your nearest Magistrate's Court is designed to be used without a lawyer: the limit is R30,000 (raised from R20,000 with effect from 1 August 2026), neither side may bring an attorney into the hearing, any official language may be used, and claims against the state are excluded. You may claim a larger debt there only by permanently abandoning the amount above the limit. Allow the waiting period after the letter of demand before you proceed — the Department of Justice states 14 days, Legal Aid South Africa's guide says 14 working days, so waiting the longer period is safe. There is no appeal from the Small Claims Court, only a narrow review. For companies, larger or disputed debts, or repeat offenders, weigh an attorney's letter and Magistrates' Court action (or a registered debt collector) against the size of the debt.

## What to expect and how to measure it
Track three numbers monthly: debtor days (how long, on average, your money takes to arrive), the share of invoices more than 14 days overdue, and the age analysis buckets (current, 30, 60, 90+). The goal is each number falling quarter by quarter. No credible source supports promising a specific percentage improvement in a specific time, so this entry does not; the reliable effect of the ladder is that fewer invoices age into the expensive rungs.

## Common mistakes and when to get help
The classic mistakes: continuing to supply a non-payer; never agreeing terms in writing and losing the right to contractual interest; charging an arbitrary "penalty" rate no agreement supports; a company trying to sue in the Small Claims Court (it cannot — claimants must be natural persons); abandoning the excess above the court's limit without realising the abandonment is permanent; and invalid invoices to government that never start the 30-day clock.

Use an attorney for debts above the Small Claims limit, disputed debts, drafting your credit terms, and anything against the state; use an accountant to set up the age analysis and reminder automation. Interest calculations are technical enough that a worked figure should be checked by a professional before you sue for it. InfoDot shares experience; it does not replace legal advice.
