A business run through the owner's personal bank account cannot say what it earns, cannot complete its own statutory filings, and cannot defend a tax return. Separating the money takes an afternoon; keeping honest books after that is a monthly routine of three verbs — capture, reconcile, review — that an owner can run without an accountant.

## The problem
Mixed money looks harmless until a form asks a question only clean books can answer. SARS requires records that support every figure in every return, keeps the right to inspect them, and expects them kept in an orderly, safe form — reconstructing a year of business activity out of a personal account's groceries and school fees is slow at best and impossible at worst. CIPC's annual return itself demands a turnover figure and financial statements or the Financial Accountability Supplement, so a business that cannot compute its turnover cannot even complete its statutory filing. And a company is a separate taxpayer from its owner: personal spending out of company funds has tax consequences the books must be able to explain. There is no honest statistic on how many businesses die of mixed money, so this entry quotes none — the regulatory consequences above are verified and are enough.

## Who this is for
Every trading sole proprietor (your personal return must separate business income and expenses) and every private company (its money is legally not yours). If you employ people you also carry payroll records. The core audience is the owner doing their own books.

## Step 1 — Open a dedicated business account
For a company, open the account with the CIPC documents (BizPortal can start one with a big-four bank during registration). For a sole proprietor, a second account in your own name is sufficient in practice — no statute forces it, but every rand of business money flowing through one dedicated account is what makes the rest of this entry a routine instead of an archaeology project.

## Step 2 — Pay yourself on purpose, not by swipe
Move a fixed amount to your personal account on a fixed day — a salary from a company, drawings for a sole proprietor — and spend personally only from the personal side. Ad-hoc spending from the business account is the single habit that re-mixes the money.

## Step 3 — Set up the records system SARS expects
Keep every source document — invoices issued and received, receipts, bank statements, contracts — in an orderly fashion in a safe place, open to inspection. Electronic records are allowed in the form SARS prescribes; storing records outside South Africa, or in another form, needs prior SARS authorisation (section 30(2) of the Tax Administration Act, applied for on form EFR001). A labelled folder per month, paper or digital, passes this test; a shoebox does not.

## Step 4 — Monthly: capture
Once a month, record every transaction in your ledger, spreadsheet or accounting app, each entry filed against its source document. Capturing monthly from documents beats capturing at year-end from memory — the year-end version is where invented numbers come from.

## Step 5 — Monthly: reconcile
Tick every book entry against the bank statement, line by line. Investigate anything unmatched. Log the exceptions — the cash sale, the business cost that hit a personal card — and bring them into the books explicitly. A reconciled month is closed; you never have to think about it again.

## Step 6 — Monthly: review
Read three numbers: income versus expenses for the month; the rolling 12-month turnover (watching the R2.3 million compulsory VAT threshold, as at October 2026); and what the business owes and is owed. Then check the tax money — provisional tax, VAT if registered, PAYE if you employ — is actually set aside, not resting in the spending balance.

## Step 7 — Keep the archive five years
SARS requires records kept for five years from the date the return was submitted; where a required return was never filed, the duty runs indefinitely until it is; and records must be kept longer while an audit, objection or appeal is running. Employers keep Compensation Fund records four years. Design the archive once — year, then month, then document — and the five-year rule costs nothing.

## Step 8 — Year-end handover
Clean monthly books make year-end mechanical: the figures for the tax return, the CIPC annual return's turnover, and the financial statements come straight out of the file. Hand the professional a reconciled file, not a box, and you pay for judgement instead of sorting.

## Tools and resources
The SARS small-business pages — "Starting a business and tax" and "Record keeping" at sars.gov.za — are free and official. SARS eFiling for every filing; the CIPC annual-return portal's fee calculator at annualreturns.cipc.co.za. No specific software is legally required — SARS prescribes the form of records, not a product — so a bank-statement download plus a spreadsheet is a legitimate system; accounting apps add automation when volume justifies it. Free business advice through SEDA/SEDFA offices.

## How you know it is working
The account reconciles to the books every month with zero unexplained items; owner pay leaves as regular transfers; you can state last month's income and expenses and the rolling 12-month turnover on demand; the archive is complete for five years; filings go out on time; and the tax money is reserved before it is due.

## Common mistakes and when to get help
One account for everything; capturing at year-end from memory; discarding slips once a return is filed (the five-year rule runs from submission); cloud-only or offshore storage without checking the SARS form requirements; and treating company money as personal because "it is all mine anyway" — in law it is not.

Bring in a professional when you approach the VAT threshold, when you first employ, for a SARS audit, objection or appeal, and for company annual financial statements (whether a company needs an audit or independent review depends on Companies Act criteria — confirm for your company). Anyone you pay for tax work must be registered with a Recognised Controlling Body and with SARS as a tax practitioner. InfoDot shares experience; it does not replace an accountant.
