The useful business plan is one page, drafted in a sitting, and reviewed monthly — not the forty-page document written once for a funder and then shelved. The research supports exactly this: in a study of about 1,000 founders, those who wrote a plan were 16% more likely to get their venture to viability, the benefit peaked at roughly three months of planning effort, and planning done while actually talking to customers was worth the most. The plan is a set of dated claims you keep testing, not an essay.

## The problem
Traditional plans run to dozens of pages (the US Small Business Administration's own description) and suit one situation: seeking formal finance. Written too early or polished too long, they go stale — the same study found benefits faded past about three months of planning because the information in the plan became outdated. So most owners either write nothing, or write the long version once and never open it again. Both lose the real value of planning, which is forcing the business's assumptions into daylight and checking them against reality on a schedule.

## Who this is for
New founders; existing small businesses that have never written anything down; and owners preparing for funding — who should still start with the one-pager and expand to the traditional format only when a lender requires it.

## The approach and why it works
A one-page lean plan plus a booked monthly review. Evidence: research-backed — Greene & Hopp tracked about 1,000 US founders (Panel Study of Entrepreneurial Dynamics II, 2005–2011): formal planners were 16% more likely to achieve viability; around three months of planning was optimal (+12%); and planning done alongside customer conversations and getting the product ready was associated with a 27% higher chance of viability. The one-page format is documented practice: the SBA formally describes the lean plan ("as little as one hour to make", typically one page), and its nine blocks mirror the Business Model Canvas by Alexander Osterwalder (Strategyzer), developed with Yves Pigneur. The monthly review discipline is expert recommendation (Tim Berry, who built a career on business planning: the process matters more than the document).

## Step 1 — Block out one working session
A first draft takes about an hour (SBA's own estimate). Do not book a week.

## Step 2 — Draft the nine blocks
On one page: customer segments, value proposition, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partnerships, cost structure. Short phrases, not sentences. Strategyzer's official Business Model Canvas PDF is a free download if you want the classic layout.

## Step 3 — Sharpen the value proposition per segment
For each customer segment: what problem you solve, for whom, against what they currently use instead. If this box is vague, nothing downstream can be sharp.

## Step 4 — Put real numbers in costs and revenue
Fixed and variable costs, your prices, and the break-even check from the pricing entry: fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit). A one-page plan with no numbers is a poster.

## Step 5 — Mark the riskiest assumption in each block
Date and version the page, and treat it, in Strategyzer's words, as a claim rather than a plan. The riskiest assumptions — "restaurants will pay monthly", "the supplier can deliver weekly" — are your to-do list.

## Step 6 — Test assumptions with real customer conversations
This is where the study's biggest effect lives: planning done while talking to customers and readying the product carried the 27% viability advantage. Every month, pick the riskiest untested assumption and go ask real customers about it. (The finding-your-first-customers entry covers how.)

## Step 7 — Book the monthly review
One to two hours, fixed date, even for a one-person business: compare the plan's numbers with what actually happened, update the assumptions, save the new version. This standing meeting is the difference between a plan and a souvenir.

## Step 8 — Expand only when a funder requires it
The traditional format — executive summary, company description, market analysis, organisation, product line, marketing and sales, funding request, financial projections — exists for lenders and formal investors (the SBA documents it fully, with examples). Write it from your living one-pager when asked, not before.

## What to expect and how to measure it
First draft in about an hour; monthly review in one to two. The research supports improved odds — 16% from planning at all, more when timed with customer contact — not guaranteed success, and the gains came from planning at the right time, not from longer documents. Track two things: assumptions tested per month, and the gap between planned and actual revenue and costs shrinking over time.

## Common mistakes and when to get help
Writing the plan before talking to a single customer; polishing for months (returns fade past about three); treating the page as a finished artefact instead of a dated, versioned claim; and sending a one-pager to a bank that asked for the traditional format. Use an accountant for the financial projections when funding is on the table; SEDA and SCORE advice is free. InfoDot shares experience; it does not replace an adviser.
