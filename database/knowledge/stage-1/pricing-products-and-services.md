Most small businesses price by copying a competitor or by what "feels reasonable" — which is guessing. The method here builds a verified cost floor (direct costs, overhead, and the owner's time at a market rate), checks it against break-even, then prices deliberately between that floor and what the product is worth to a specific customer. Price is the strongest profit lever a business has: in a Harvard Business Review study of 2,463 large companies, a 1% price improvement raised operating profit by 11.1% on average — assuming no volume is lost — against 3.3% for the same improvement in volume.

## The problem
Prices set without knowing the cost base silently lose money on every sale. SCORE, the US small-business mentoring body, warns that owners typically price from competitors or gut feel, that copying competitors often produces losses (their costs are not your costs), and that without the cost base pricing is guesswork. No honest statistic exists for how many businesses underprice, so this entry quotes none — but the mechanics below show you within an afternoon whether you are one of them.

## Who this is for
Small product and service businesses, freelancers and consultants setting an hourly or project rate, and South African businesses showing prices to consumers (VAT display rules apply — step 7).

## The approach and why it works
Cost floor first, then a deliberate choice of method. Knowing your full cost before choosing a price is industry-recognised best practice (SCORE, Corporate Finance Institute); the leverage of getting price right is research-backed (Marn & Rosiello, "Managing Price, Gaining Profit", HBR 1992 — the 11.1% figure above, a large-company average, not a promise); the conditions where value-based pricing works are expert recommendation (Utpal Dholakia, HBR 2016).

## Step 1 — Total your direct costs per unit or job
Materials, direct labour, delivery, payment fees — everything that happens only because this sale happens. This is the variable cost per unit.

## Step 2 — Total your annual overhead
Rent, insurance, equipment, marketing, software, accounting — everything you pay whether or not a sale happens.

## Step 3 — Cost your own time at a market rate
The commonest hole in a small business's cost base is the owner working free. Price your labour at what you would pay someone else to do it (salary benchmarks are free on sites like Payscale), add roughly 30–35% for tax and benefits, then divide by realistic billable hours: start from 2,080 hours a year (52 weeks × 40 hours), subtract leave, admin, selling and training — large consultancies target 70–80% billable; a solo operator is usually lower. Breakeven rate = total annual costs ÷ billable hours; your rate = breakeven × (1 + a profit markup, typically 10–30%). Worked shape: needing R600,000 a year over 1,200 billable hours means R500 an hour is the floor, before profit.

## Step 4 — Never confuse margin with markup
Margin is profit over PRICE; markup is profit over COST. Same sale, price R100, cost R70: that is a 30% margin but a 42.9% markup. Markup is always the bigger number, so applying a "30% markup" when the business needs a 30% margin silently under-recovers profit on every sale. To hit a target margin: price = cost ÷ (1 − margin). For a 30% margin on a R70 cost: 70 ÷ 0.7 = R100.

## Step 5 — Check break-even against reality
Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit). Corporate Finance Institute's example: R100,000 of fixed costs at a R12 price and R2 variable cost means 10,000 units to break even. If the break-even volume is more than you can realistically sell, the price — or the cost base — is wrong, and no amount of effort fixes the arithmetic.

## Step 6 — Choose the pricing method on purpose
Cost-plus (price = total cost × (1 + markup)) is simple and guarantees the floor but ignores what customers would pay. Competitive pricing is only safe when your costs are genuinely comparable to the competitor you are copying. Value-based pricing — pricing on what the product is worth to a specific segment against their next-best alternative — earns the most, but per Dholakia it needs a defined segment and sensibly-priced competition, and it cannot rescue a structurally low price. Many businesses run cost-plus as the floor and value-based on their top products.

## Step 7 — South African VAT on displayed prices
The standard VAT rate is 15% (the announced 2025 increase was reversed; rate maintained at 15% — as at October 2026 on sars.gov.za). Section 65 of the VAT Act requires a vendor's advertised or quoted prices to include VAT, or to show the inclusive and exclusive price with equal prominence. Inclusive price = exclusive price × 1.15. VAT registration is compulsory from R2.3 million of taxable supplies in 12 months (as at October 2026); if you are not registered, you charge no VAT.

## Step 8 — Raise prices without losing the room
Announce in advance (established practice runs to 90 days' notice for business customers); call it a price increase, not an "adjustment" — euphemisms erode trust; give a short honest reason and the effective date; do not apologise or sound tentative; treat all customers consistently. Softeners that work: grandfather loyal clients for a defined period, or introduce a good/better/best tier so customers choose their price point.

## What to expect and how to measure it
Watch gross margin percent and contribution per unit monthly, break-even units against actual sales, and (for services) your billable utilisation. Do not expect the study's 11.1% as a personal guarantee — it is a large-company average that assumes no volume loss; your own result depends on how customers respond, which is why increases are tested and announced, not sprung.

## Common mistakes and when to get help
Confusing markup with margin; copying a competitor's price without knowing your own costs; leaving the owner's salary, the 30–35% tax-and-benefits loading, or non-billable time out of a rate; advertising VAT-exclusive prices to consumers (a section 65 breach); apologising your way through an increase; and expecting value-based pricing to fix a price that was structurally too low.
