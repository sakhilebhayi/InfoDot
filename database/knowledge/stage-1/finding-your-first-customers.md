A new business's first customers are almost never found by advertising — they are recruited by hand, one conversation at a time. This entry is the method for going from zero to roughly your first ten to fifty paying customers with no marketing budget: define one narrow target, research it cheaply, recruit manually through warm introductions and direct outreach, then turn satisfied customers into referrals.

## The problem
Three habits keep new businesses at zero customers. First, the target market is undefined or too broad — chasing two customer segments with different sales processes splits scarce time and money, a warning MIT entrepreneurship lecturer Paul Cheek makes in the US Chamber of Commerce's guidance. Second, founders wait for customers to come to them: Paul Graham's essay "Do Things That Don't Scale" calls manual recruitment the most common unscalable work founders avoid — out of shyness, or because the early numbers look too small to matter. Third, no research: the US Small Business Administration's guide lists the questions — is there demand, how many buyers, where are they, who already serves them, what will they pay — that are guesses until you ask.

## Who this is for
Any new or very small business, service or product, township or online, from registration to the first few dozen paying customers. If you already have steady customers and want more of them, the customer-retention entry is the companion to this one.

## The approach and why it works
Narrow targeting plus manual recruitment plus referrals. It works because at zero customers your advantage is doing what bigger businesses cannot: personal attention. Airbnb's founders went door to door in New York recruiting hosts one at a time; Stripe's founders set new users up on the spot instead of emailing a link (both documented in Graham's essay). The referral step works because, as Cheek puts it, your best salespeople are your customers. This is documented real-world practice and expert recommendation, not controlled research — the sources are cited below.

## Step 1 — Write down one narrow target customer
One sentence: who they are, their situation, and what they currently pay for instead of you. One segment only. "Everyone" is not a target; neither are two segments with different buying processes.

## Step 2 — Answer the research questions cheaply
Work through the SBA's questions — demand, number of buyers, where they are, who else serves them, what they pay — using free existing data and, above all, direct conversations with ten or so people in the segment. You are checking that the problem is real and finding out where these buyers actually gather.

## Step 3 — Mine your warm network
List every person who is, or can introduce you to, your target customer. Ask each one for a specific introduction ("do you know a school principal I could show this to?"), not a vague share. Warm introductions convert far better than cold anything.

## Step 4 — Send short, personal, direct messages
Build a list of prospects who look like your target and contact them personally — short, specific, about their problem, not your product. In South Africa that realistically includes WhatsApp: it is the country's most used platform, with 93.9% of active social media users on it (Global Digital Report 2024 figures via Meltwater), and the free WhatsApp Business app gives you a catalogue and quick replies. Test a few wordings and channels; keep what gets responses.

## Step 5 — Go where buyers already gather
Community groups, industry events, markets, the places your research found. Do one-on-one demonstrations. Pinterest's founder recruited early users personally at a design conference (Graham); the principle scales down to a stall at the Saturday market.

## Step 6 — Make saying yes effortless
Close on the spot: set the customer up, take the first order, book the first job there and then, rather than sending something to look at later. Every delay between interest and first value loses customers.

## Step 7 — Deliver visibly well, then ask for the referral
Over-deliver for the first customers — they are your proof. Then ask each satisfied one for a referral and a testimonial, specifically and soon after the win. Referrals keep your acquisition cost near zero.

## Step 8 — Repeat what worked, drop what did not
Review which experiments produced actual paying customers — not likes, customers — and put your hours into those. Run small, cheap experiments before any spend on expensive channels.

## Tools and resources
The SBA's free market-research framework (sba.gov, Business Guide); SCORE's free mentoring and first-customers resources (score.org); in South Africa, SEDA's free business-development support (seda.org.za, call centre 0860 103 703); the free WhatsApp Business app; and Paul Graham's essay "Do Things That Don't Scale" (paulgraham.com/ds.html) — twenty minutes of reading that reframes the whole stage.

## What to expect and how to measure it
No credible source gives universal first-customer benchmarks, so this entry promises none. Track four numbers weekly: conversations started, demonstrations given, customers won per channel, and referrals asked for versus received. Expect small absolute numbers — Graham's arithmetic is that growing 10% a week from a tiny base compounds to thousands within a year, so early smallness is normal, not failure.

## Common mistakes and when to get help
Targeting everyone, or two segments at once; waiting for inbound interest instead of recruiting; spending on radio, print or boosted posts before cheap experiments have found a working channel; over-promising to early customers — poor delivery to your first customers cancels the referral engine and spreads the opposite of word of mouth; and skipping research, which usually surfaces later as mispricing.

Free help exists before you pay anyone: SEDA branches and Business Partners' resources in South Africa, SCORE mentors elsewhere. Pay a marketing agency only once you know, from your own experiments, which channel it should amplify.
