A business can show a profit on paper and still miss payroll, because profit and cash are not the same thing. A rolling 13-week cash flow forecast is a one-page spreadsheet that tells you, week by week for the next quarter, whether there will be money in the bank — early enough to fix a gap before it becomes a crisis. Building the first one takes about a day; keeping it alive takes a short session every week.

## The problem
The income statement says the business made money, but on a given Friday there is not enough in the bank for wages, rent, a supplier who wants cash on delivery, or the VAT payment. The owner finds out on the day, when the only options left are expensive.

This happens for three reasons. First, most small businesses hold very thin cash buffers: a JPMorgan Chase Institute study of 597,000 small businesses (2016) found the median business held only 27 "buffer days" — enough cash to cover about four weeks of normal outflows if money stopped coming in — and a quarter held less than two weeks. Second, profit is recorded when you invoice, but cash arrives when the customer actually pays, while wages, rent and tax leave on fixed dates. Third, in South Africa late payment is structural: National Treasury's own compliance tracking showed 95,399 government invoices older than 30 days, worth R12.4 billion, unpaid at the end of the second quarter of 2025.

None of this shows up in an income statement. It only shows up in a week-by-week picture of the bank account.

## Who this is for
Any owner-managed business with real money moving every week: trades and services, retail, construction, manufacturing, suppliers to corporates or government. It matters most where there is payroll, seasonal income, customers who pay slowly, or thin margins. A pre-revenue startup should model its runway instead; this entry is for operating businesses.

## The approach and why it works
Keep a direct cash forecast with one column per week for the next 13 weeks (one quarter): cash in, cash out, and the resulting bank balance, updated every week by dropping the week just finished and adding a new week 13 at the end — which is why it is called rolling.

Thirteen weeks is the standard horizon because it is long enough to see a problem coming and short enough to stay accurate week by week. This exact model is what lenders, acquirers and turnaround practitioners ask for when a company's cash is under pressure — it is the standard liquidity tool of corporate restructuring — and professional bodies such as ACCA recommend the same discipline for small businesses: forecast ahead, then compare what actually happened against the forecast at least monthly, ideally weekly. The weekly comparison is what makes your numbers honest over time.

## Step 1 — Set up the sheet and the true opening balance
Open a spreadsheet. Columns: the next 13 weeks, with the current week as week 1. Rows in three blocks: cash coming in, cash going out, and balances (opening balance, net movement, closing balance).

Then enter the true opening cash position: the reconciled bank balance — today's bank statement balance, plus deposits already on their way in, minus payments you have released that have not yet cleared. If this first number is wrong, every week after it is wrong.

## Step 2 — Map the cash coming in
Work through your list of unpaid invoices (the debtors book) and place each one in the week you realistically expect the money — based on how that customer has actually paid before, not on the due date printed on the invoice. A customer who always pays at 45 days goes in at 45 days. Add cash sales as a weekly average from recent history. Be pessimistic about new customers until they have a payment record.

## Step 3 — Map the cash going out
List payments week by week from the creditors book plus the fixed diary: wages and the PAYE/UIF payment date, rent, insurance, loan instalments, and tax on its statutory due dates — VAT and provisional tax are the most commonly forgotten lines and among the largest. Include the owner's drawings as a planned line, not a surprise.

## Step 4 — Read the closing-balance line and act on gaps
For each week: net movement = cash in minus cash out; closing balance = opening balance plus net movement; each closing balance becomes the next week's opening. The result is a line of 13 bank balances.

Any week where that line goes below zero — or below your own minimum, such as one payroll — is the problem to work on now, while there is still time. The levers, in order: chase specific debtors whose money would land before the gap; move a discretionary payment later; agree a payment arrangement with a supplier; arrange an overdraft or facility before you need it. Banks respond far better to an owner who arrives weeks early with a forecast than to one who calls on the day.

## Step 5 — Make it a weekly routine and keep it rolling
Fix a weekly slot — for example Monday morning. Enter last week's actual receipts and payments next to what you forecast, look at the differences, and correct your assumptions (that customer really pays at 60 days; electricity is higher in winter). Then delete the finished week, add a new week 13, and update the lot. A forecast built once and left to age is decoration; the weekly roll is the whole method.

## Tools and resources
A plain spreadsheet (Google Sheets or Excel) is enough; your accounting package's age analysis of debtors and creditors is the data feed. Free templates: Financial Edge Training publishes a free 13-week cash flow Excel template (fe.training, under free resources, restructuring), and SCORE, the US small-business mentoring body, keeps free cash-flow projection templates at score.org/resource/templates.

## What to expect and how to measure it
Two numbers tell you whether this is working. First, cash buffer days: your average bank balance divided by your average daily cash outflows. In the JPMorgan Chase Institute study the median small business held 27 buffer days; below about 13 days was the bottom quarter. Growing this number is the goal. Second, forecast accuracy: each week, how far out was last week's forecast? It should shrink month by month as your assumptions get honest. No credible source promises a specific improvement in a specific time, so this entry does not either — what the forecast reliably buys you is warning time.

## Common mistakes and when to get help
The usual failures: starting from an unreconciled bank figure; placing receipts on invoice due dates instead of customers' real paying behaviour; leaving VAT, PAYE and provisional tax off the sheet; taking irregular personal drawings that the forecast never sees; building the forecast once and never rolling it; and hiding it from the bank until the crisis.

Bring in an accountant when the forecast shows a gap you cannot close by shifting debtors and creditors, when you need to apply for a facility, or when tax arrears are building — in that case talk to SARS early through a tax practitioner rather than letting the debt age.
