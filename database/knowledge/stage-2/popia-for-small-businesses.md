POPIA has applied in full since 1 July 2021, and it covers every business that processes personal information — customer lists, staff files, CCTV, a WhatsApp order group. There is no small-business exemption. The good news: for a small business, real compliance is about ten concrete artefacts, most of them one page each, and the Regulator's enforcement record shows it punishes ignoring problems and notices — not honest small operators with a working programme. This entry is that programme.

## The problem
Three myths do the damage. "We're too small for POPIA" — the Act binds any responsible party, and the Information Regulator's own position on Information Officer registration is that it "is not optional". "Consent fixes everything" — consent is only one of six legal grounds (section 11), it must be provable (the burden is yours), and chasing consent where a contract or legitimate interest already covers you creates work and risk for nothing. And "an unsubscribe link means they agreed" — for electronic direct marketing, an opt-out is not consent; section 69 permits marketing by SMS/email/automated calls only to people who consented or are existing customers, with strict conditions.

## Who this is for
Every South African business holding any personal information — which is every business with customers, staff or suppliers who are people.

## Step 1 — Know who your Information Officer is, and register them
By default it's the head of the business: the owner (sole proprietor), any partner, or the CEO/equivalent — who may authorise someone else in writing while keeping accountability (the Regulator's guidance note). The officer must register with the Regulator BEFORE taking up duties (POPIA s55(2)), on the free eServices portal (eservices.inforegulator.org.za). Fifteen minutes of admin; it is also the login you'll need if you ever report a breach.

## Step 2 — Make the one-page data inventory
List what personal information you hold, where it lives, why you collect it, who it's shared with, and how long you keep it. This single table feeds almost every other duty: purpose specification (collect for a specific purpose), retention (don't keep it longer than necessary — then delete, destroy or de-identify, s14), and openness.

## Step 3 — Pick the legal ground per purpose (consent last)
Section 11's grounds: consent; necessary for a contract with the person; a legal obligation; protecting the person's legitimate interest; a public-law duty; or your (or a third party's) legitimate interests. Invoicing a customer rides on contract; keeping payroll records rides on legal obligation; consent is for what nothing else covers — and where you use it, keep the proof and honour withdrawal.

## Step 4 — Publish a plain privacy notice
Section 18's items in human language: what you collect, why, who you are and how to reach you, whether supplying it is voluntary or required and what happens if not, who it's shared with, the person's rights of access and correction, and the right to complain to the Information Regulator. One page on your website and at the till beats ten pages of copied legalese that doesn't match what you actually do.

## Step 5 — Safeguards and operator contracts
Section 19 requires "appropriate, reasonable technical and organisational measures", regularly verified, with regard to generally accepted security practice — the practical baseline is the standard small-business security set (unique passwords + two-step verification, updates, tested backups, access removed when staff leave; InfoDot's cybersecurity-baseline entry is the companion to this one). Section 21 adds the piece most small businesses miss: a WRITTEN contract with every operator — payroll bureau, cloud software, IT support, marketing service — obliging them to the same safeguards and to notify you immediately of any suspected compromise. Established vendors have a data-processing agreement ready to sign; its absence is a warning.

## Step 6 — Write the one-page breach procedure before you need it
The section 22 drill: where there are reasonable grounds to believe personal information was accessed or acquired by an unauthorised person, notify the Regulator AND the affected people, in writing, as soon as reasonably possible — the Act sets no 72-hour clock (that's GDPR), but the Regulator's guidance says report on what you know and update later rather than waiting for the investigation, and reporting goes through the eServices portal (mandatory channel since April 2025). Your one-pager: who decides it's a breach (the Information Officer), who holds the portal login, the notification template, and the rule that an operator's breach is still YOUR notification to make.

## Step 7 — Fix direct marketing properly
For electronic channels (email, SMS, automated calls): market only to people who consented — consent may be requested ONCE from someone who hasn't previously refused, on the Regulator's Form 4 or any equally clear recorded manner per the amended Regulations (April 2025) — or to existing customers, and then only for your own similar products, where an opt-out was offered at collection and appears in EVERY message. Every message must identify you and give a working opt-out contact (s69(4)). The Regulator's first direct-marketing enforcement actions exist, and the fined parties include a small consultancy — this is the POPIA provision most likely to touch a small business first.

## Step 8 — Compile the PAIA manual (the exemption is gone)
Every private body needs a PAIA manual; the old exemption for small private bodies expired on 31 December 2021. The Regulator publishes a fill-in template for private bodies (on inforegulator.org.za) — completing it honestly takes an hour. Keep it on your website or available at your premises.

## Step 9 — Train the team and set retention dates
A one-hour session covering: what counts as personal information, the privacy notice, who the Information Officer is, the marketing rules, and "if something leaks, tell the officer the same day". Add delete-by dates to the Step 2 inventory and act on them — indefinite retention is itself non-compliance (s14).

## What to expect and how to measure it
The honest enforcement picture (the Regulator's statements and reported actions through late 2026): administrative fines observed range from R100,000 to R5 million — overwhelmingly against public bodies, with one bank fined R1 million — and the pattern is that fines follow ignored enforcement notices, unreported breaches and repeat failures, not first honest mistakes. The statutory ceiling is R10 million and certain obstruction offences carry criminal liability, but a small business with the nine artefacts above (registered officer, inventory, grounds, notice, safeguards, operator contracts, breach procedure, compliant marketing, PAIA manual) is in the defensible position the Act actually asks for. Measure it as a checklist: nine items, each either exists or doesn't.

## Common mistakes and when to get help
Treating registration as optional; consent harvesting where contract already covers the processing; opt-out treated as opt-in; no operator contracts with the cloud services that actually hold the data; breach silence (the Regulator's enforcement notices explicitly cite failing to notify under s22); marketing lists bought from brokers; keeping everything forever; and copying a corporate privacy policy that promises processes you don't have. Get a privacy attorney for: special personal information (health, biometrics, children's data), cross-border transfers at scale, or anything involving an Enforcement Committee letter. For the rest, the Regulator's own guidance notes and templates are free and current: inforegulator.org.za, enquiries@inforegulator.org.za, 080 001 7160.
