Every business that depends on one person's memory is fragile: quality varies by who worked that day, training takes months of shadowing, and mistakes repeat. Standard operating procedures fix that — but only the ones people actually use. Most SOPs die in a folder. This entry is the method for writing ones that live: choose few, write them with the people who do the work, put them where the work happens, and treat them as living documents. The evidence is sobering in a useful way: checklists produced dramatic results where they came with culture and feedback, and nothing where they were just imposed — so the method matters more than the document.

## The problem
Three failure modes, straight from the agencies that study this. SOPs that exist but aren't followed — the US EPA's SOP guidance is blunt that even well-written SOPs fail without management reviewing and reinforcing their use, and with no current copy accessible where the work is done. SOPs pitched at the wrong level — too vague to reproduce the task, or so detailed nobody reads them (EPA: the detail should let someone with limited experience do the task unsupervised, no more). And the deeper trap: believing the document alone changes behaviour. When Ontario mandated surgical checklists across 101 hospitals, mortality and complications did not significantly improve (Urbach et al., NEJM 2014); in the BetterBirth trial, checklist adherence jumped with coaching but decayed within a year and outcomes didn't move (Semrau et al., NEJM 2017). Documents don't change work — the rollout does.

## Who this is for
Any business where more than one person does (or will do) the same task: food preparation, deliveries, month-end invoicing, machine setup, client onboarding. Especially businesses that currently live in the owner's head.

## Step 1 — Choose the first five processes deliberately
Don't document everything; document what pays. Choose processes that affect many people, tie up real money, cross hand-off boundaries, generate repeated questions or corrections, or must be done right when nobody senior is around (selection criteria from the University of Würzburg's process-documentation programme; NASA's checklist research adds: protect the critical "killer" items above all). A good first five for most small businesses: opening/closing routines, the highest-risk safety or hygiene task, invoicing and payment capture, the core service delivery, and handling a customer complaint.

## Step 2 — Write it with the person who does the work
The EPA's rule: SOPs should be written by the people who actually do the work — a team approach creates buy-in, and the writer's job is to watch the task done for real and capture it. Write short, active-voice, numbered steps; add a simple flowchart where the process branches or hands off. One page where possible. Aim the detail at a competent newcomer, not at the expert who wrote it.

## Step 3 — Pick the right format for the task
Two proven shapes, from aviation research (NASA's flight-deck checklist study) as popularised in medicine: **do-confirm** — do the work from skill, then pause and verify the critical items (keeps experienced people fast, catches omissions); and **read-do** — read a step, do the step, for tasks where order matters, stakes are high, or the person is new. Put the most critical items early (NASA: interruptions kill the tail of a list), chunk long procedures into smaller task-lists, and never let the checklist become a discipline tool — NASA's research is explicit that checklists are highly vulnerable to production pressure and to being resented.

## Step 4 — Test it cold before approving it
Have someone OTHER than the writer perform the task using only the draft (EPA calls this especially helpful). Every place they stall or improvise is a defect in the document, not in them. Fix, then approve.

## Step 5 — Version-control it like you mean it
Each SOP carries a control block on every page: short title, ID, revision number, date, page x of y (EPA). Keep a master list of all SOPs with owner, version and review date; archive old versions so they cannot keep being used; withdraw SOPs for processes you no longer follow. Review each SOP on a schedule — every one to two years (EPA) — and immediately when the process changes. Keep the review light, or it won't happen.

## Step 6 — Roll out like the successes, not the failures
What distinguished the famous wins from the null results was everything around the paper. The Michigan ICU programme (103 ICUs) paired a five-item line-insertion checklist with the authority for ANY staff member to stop a non-compliant procedure, plus continuous feedback of each unit's infection rates — median infection rates fell from 2.7 per 1,000 catheter-days to zero within three months and stayed down at three years (Pronovost et al., NEJM 2006; BMJ 2010). The later analysis (Dixon-Woods et al., Milbank Quarterly 2011) credited the social system — peer pressure, shared commitment, data as a mirror — not the checklist alone. Translate that to a small business: put the SOP at the point of work; train it at onboarding; have a named owner reinforce it; measure something the SOP should move (error rate, redo rate, complaint rate) and show the numbers to the team; and give everyone licence to call a skipped step. Expect roughly 60–90 days of consistent reinforcement before a procedure becomes habit (the FDA's retail food-safety manual's rule of thumb for control systems).

## Step 7 — Keep one honest scoreboard
Pick one or two numbers per SOP and track them monthly: mistakes caught, customer complaints on that process, time to train a newcomer, audit pass rate (the EPA's own trick: use the SOP itself as the audit checklist). If the number doesn't move in a quarter, the SOP or the rollout is wrong — revise with the team rather than laminating it harder.

## What to expect and how to measure it
Honest expectations: where checklists plus culture were deployed, results were dramatic (Michigan; the WHO surgical checklist's 8-hospital study saw deaths fall from 1.5% to 0.8% and complications from 11% to 7% — Haynes et al., NEJM 2009); where documents were simply mandated, nothing happened (Ontario). Your measurable targets: all first-five SOPs tested-cold and versioned within six weeks; every new hire trained from SOPs, not shadowing alone; the chosen error metric visibly falling within a quarter.

## Common mistakes and when to get help
Documenting everything at once instead of the paying five; SOPs written by the boss alone at a desk; the checklist treated as the whole SOP (it's a component — EPA); no version control, so old copies circulate; reviews so heavy they never happen; using the list to police people; ignoring production pressure — the busiest day is when steps get skipped, so the critical items must be few and early; and declaring victory on adoption instead of outcomes (the BetterBirth lesson). Professional help is rarely needed for SOPs themselves; where a regulated domain applies (food safety, health and safety), start from the regulator's own required controls and build your SOPs around them.
