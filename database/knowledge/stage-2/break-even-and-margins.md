"Are we actually making money?" has an exact, teachable answer, and it takes an afternoon with a calculator: know your contribution margin, compute your break-even, keep a margin of safety, and never confuse markup with margin — the single arithmetic slip that quietly underprices thousands of businesses. This entry is that toolkit, with a worked rent decision, because the most common use of break-even in a small business is "can I afford premises?"

## The problem
Three numeric blind spots. Owners price by markup but think in margin: a 50% markup is only a 33.3% margin — marking up cost by 25% while believing you earn "25% margin" (you earn 20%) compounds into real money (a worked university example shows a 36% markup versus a 36% margin gap of nearly $3.9m on $19.2m of cost). Owners don't know their break-even, so they can't tell a good month from a slow bleed. And costs get mis-sorted — semi-variable costs lumped as fixed, annual costs forgotten — which the SBA's guidance addresses directly: convert everything to monthly, split semi-variable costs, and add roughly 10% for the unpredictable items.

## Who this is for
Every business, but urgently: anyone setting prices, anyone considering premises, staff or equipment (anything that raises fixed costs), and anyone whose "profit" only exists because their own salary isn't in the costs.

## Step 1 — Sort your costs honestly
List every monthly cost. FIXED: rent, salaries, insurance, interest, depreciation, subscriptions — what you pay regardless of sales. VARIABLE: what each sale consumes — materials, cost of goods, transaction fees, per-job transport, sales commission. Convert annual or quarterly items to monthly; split airtime-style semi-variable costs into their fixed and usage parts; add the SBA's ~10% buffer for miscellaneous. If you are a VAT vendor, work entirely in ex-VAT prices and costs — the VAT portion (15% in South Africa; SARS) is collected for SARS and was never yours.

## Step 2 — Compute contribution margin per unit
Contribution margin = selling price − variable cost per unit (OpenStax managerial accounting). This is the money each sale contributes to covering fixed costs — and it differs from gross margin whenever variable selling costs (like commission) exist. The ratio version: contribution margin ÷ price.

## Step 3 — Compute break-even in units and in rand
Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin per unit. Break-even revenue = fixed costs ÷ contribution-margin ratio (SBA and OpenStax both). Example: price R50, variable cost R20 → contribution R30 (ratio 60%). Fixed costs R9,000/month → break-even = 300 units, or R15,000 of sales. Below that line every month loses money. To build a target profit in, add it to the fixed costs: wanting R6,000/month profit means (9,000+6,000)÷30 = 500 units.

## Step 4 — Know your margin of safety
Margin of safety = actual (or realistically expected) sales − break-even sales, in rand and as a percentage of sales (OpenStax). Selling R25,000 against a R15,000 break-even is a 40% cushion; selling R16,000 is a 6% cushion, and one bad week erases it. This single percentage is the honest answer to "how is business?".

## Step 5 — Run the what-if table before any big decision
Change ONE variable at a time and recompute (the OpenStax café scenarios are the template: a small price rise cuts break-even sharply; a fixed-cost rise raises it; new equipment that trims variable cost but adds fixed cost can RAISE break-even). The worked rent decision: from Step 3's business (300-unit break-even), taking premises at R6,000/month lifts fixed costs to R15,000 → break-even 500 units — 200 more units every month just to stand still. At current sales of 400 units you make R3,000/month without the shop and LOSE R3,000 with it. The question "will the premises add at least 200 units of monthly demand, reliably?" is now concrete. The same logic prices hiring: a R8,000/month employee is 8,000÷30 ≈ 267 units of new monthly sales just to break even on them. One honesty rule from the sources: the simple model assumes a price change doesn't change volume — it does, so treat results as estimates and stress-test the volume assumption.

## Step 6 — Fix the markup/margin confusion permanently
Markup is on COST; margin is on PRICE. Markup = (price − cost) ÷ cost. Margin = (price − cost) ÷ price. Conversions: margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup); to PRICE for a target margin: price = cost ÷ (1 − margin). So cost R100 with a 25% markup sells at R125 (20% margin); for a true 25% margin it must sell at R100÷0.75 = R133.33. If your business plan promises a margin, check which formula actually set your prices today.

## Step 7 — Understand your operating leverage (why rent feels risky)
Degree of operating leverage = contribution margin ÷ operating profit (OpenStax). A high-fixed-cost business multiplies swings: in the textbook's comparison, a 20% sales fall cut one firm's profit ~35% and the heavier-fixed-cost firm's ~49%. That's the arithmetic behind the instinct that premises and payroll make a business fragile — and why Step 5's cushion question matters more in a seasonal trade.

## What to expect and how to measure it
Keep three numbers on one page, updated monthly: break-even revenue, actual revenue, margin of safety %. Recompute the whole sheet whenever a price, supplier cost or fixed cost changes. Limits to respect (stated by the sources themselves): linear costs, a constant sales mix across products (work product-by-product if your mix swings), capacity bounds, and the fact that break-even is a profitability screen — not a cash-flow plan (stock bought up-front needs financing before revenue lands) and not a pricing oracle.

## Common mistakes and when to get help
Pricing by markup while reporting margin; leaving your own salary out of fixed costs (that's not profit, that's unpaid wages); mis-sorting semi-variable costs; forgetting annual costs; computing on VAT-inclusive numbers; assuming volume survives a price rise untested; one blended calculation across products with wildly different margins; and treating break-even as precise — the SBA calls it an estimate, and that's how to use it. See an accountant when allocating shared costs across product lines, when VAT registration changes your numbers, or before signing a lease the what-if table already dislikes.
